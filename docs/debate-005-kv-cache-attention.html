Debate 005

What is the KV cache, and what is attention?

Two topics, one object. The cache is not a separate invention — it is what attention forces, once you start generating.

Status: Open Draft 0.1 Opened 23 Oct 2026 Editor Divyansh Tripathi Draft · Not settled

00Framing: two questions, one object

The two are usually asked as if they were separate topics. They are not. The KV cache is not a separate invention — it is a consequence. It falls straight out of how attention works and how generation works, and it becomes, in the process, the single most important object in the economics of running an LLM.

The honest order
  1. Attention is a retrieval operation — a learned lookup.
  2. The KV cache is the memoization that autoregressive decoding makes necessary.
  3. Together they explain why inference is memory-bound, why long context is expensive, and why a whole field exists to shrink or manage one tensor.

01Attention, minimally

For each token, a self-attention layer projects its hidden state into three vectors: a query q = W_q·x, a key k = W_k·x, and a value v = W_v·x. Attention then, for each query, compares it against every key and takes a weighted average of the values:

aij = softmax( qi · kj / √d )
oi = Σj aij · vj

That is the whole operation. Read plainly, it is a retrieval: the query is a search term, the keys are an index, and the output is a soft, weighted lookup into the values. Every token asks "which earlier tokens are relevant to me?" and pulls a blend of them.

Two facts about its cost matter for everything that follows:

  • Compute is quadratic in sequence length — the score matrix is N × N.
  • It has two phases with opposite bottlenecks. Prefill (the prompt) knows all tokens at once, so it is a matrix–matrix product — compute-bound. Decode (generation) works one token at a time, so it is a matrix–vector product — memory-bound.

That last asymmetry is the hinge of the whole chapter.

02Why the cache exists

Language models generate autoregressively: token t depends on all tokens before it. To produce token t+1, the model must attend over the keys and values of tokens 1 … t. Without a cache, every step would recompute the keys and values of the entire prefix — the same vectors, over and over. Across a generation of n tokens that is O(n²) redundant work. The KV cache fixes it: store the key and value vectors as they are produced, so each step computes only the new token's k and v and attends over the cached ones.

Two properties deserve emphasis, because they are commonly misremembered:

  • The cache changes nothing about the output. It is memoization, not approximation — generation with and without it produces identical tokens. It buys speed, never accuracy.
  • It moves the cost from compute to memory. With the cache, each decode step is O(n) in attention (it must read the whole cache) and O(1) in weight matrix products. The redundant compute is gone; what remains is a large, growing tensor read on every step.

03The size — and why it is the bottleneck

The cache's size is simple to write down. For a model with L layers, h key/value heads, head dimension dh, context length S, in 16-bit:

KV bytes = 2 (keys and values) × 2 (bytes) × L × h × dh × S = 4 · L · h · dh · S

It grows linearly in context length and batch size — and it is not a rounding error. For LLaMA-3-70B (L=80, h=64, dh=128):

Context lengthKV cache (MHA)With GQA (8 KV heads)
4,096 tokens≈ 10.7 GB≈ 1.3 GB
32,768 tokens≈ 85.9 GB≈ 10.7 GB

At 32k context the MHA cache alone exceeds the 80 GB of an H100. At scale it dwarfs the weights: one analysis puts an 8B model at batch 128 and 8,192 tokens at 280 GB of MHA cache — about 30× the model's own weights (69 GB with GQA).

Now put that against the roofline. A kernel's arithmetic intensity — FLOPs per byte moved — decides whether it is compute- or memory-bound. On an H100 the ridge point is about 295 FLOP/byte. Prefill sits well above it. Decode, at small batch, sits at roughly 1 FLOP/byte — two to three orders of magnitude below the ridge. The tensor cores idle; measured GPU utilisation during decode can fall to around 7%.

The cache is not "large in the abstract." It is that decoding must re-read it every step while doing almost no math — so the cache's size, not the model's, becomes the binding constraint on batch size, throughput and context length.

04The debates: five ways to fight the cache

Everything in modern inference efficiency is, roughly, an attack on this one tensor — but the attacks are on different axes, which is why they compose.

D1 — Architecture: share the heads

The most fundamental lever: make fewer keys and values to begin with.

  • MHA (standard) one key/value head per query head.
  • MQA (Shazeer, 2019) a single shared key/value head for all queries — the cache shrinks by a factor of h, but quality suffers, and in distributed inference the single head must be replicated on every device, eroding the saving.
  • GQA (Ainslie et al., 2023) the compromise — queries are grouped, each group sharing one key/value head. Recovers most of the quality; now the default in Llama 2 70B / Llama 3, Mistral, Qwen.
  • MLA (DeepSeek) compress the hidden state into a low-rank latent and cache that, then up-project — high arithmetic intensity, at some cost to parallelizability.

The trade-off is explicit: quality vs. KV budget vs. parallelizability. The GQA paper's finding — that intermediate grouping approaches MHA quality while keeping most of MQA's benefit — is why GQA, not MQA, won.

D2 — Management: page it

PagedAttention (Debate 004) does not shrink the cache at all; it stops wasting it. Blocking the cache and mapping it through a table takes memory waste from 60–80% to under 4% and enables block-level sharing. Same tensor, better allocator.

D3 — Precision: quantize it

Store the cache in FP8 or INT4 instead of BF16 and you halve or quarter the bytes. FP8 KV cache gives a near-2× decode throughput win in the memory-bound regime. But the cautionary case is real: PyTorch found an INT4 GQA cache read 4× less data for no latency improvement at all, because the kernel was less bandwidth-efficient at INT4. Fewer bytes ≠ faster, unless the kernel can actually move them.

D4 — Algorithm: don't materialize it (FlashAttention)

This is where a widespread confusion lives. FlashAttention does not reduce the KV cache. It is exact attention that avoids ever writing the N × N score matrix to slow HBM, by tiling the computation into fast on-chip SRAM. On a GPT-2 attention block, HBM reads/writes fell from 40.3 GB to 4.4 GB, and runtime from 41.7 ms to 7.3 ms. It slashes memory traffic and peak memory — but the KV cache is unchanged. Different problem, different axis.

D5 — Approximation: the road not taken

Sparse and low-rank attention reduce the FLOPs to near-linear in sequence length. Yet many "do not display wall-clock speedup against standard attention, and have not gained wide adoption" — precisely because they optimised FLOPs and ignored memory I/O. FlashAttention's lesson was the inverse: FLOPs are not time; bytes moved are.

A sixth, systems-level trend sits above all of these: prefill/decode disaggregation — running the compute-bound prefill and the memory-bound decode on different hardware, since they stress opposite resources.

05What the evidence, read together, suggests

This is a reading, not a verdict. The editor decides what stands.

  1. Attention is retrieval; the KV cache is its memoization. The cache is exact — it never changes the output, only the speed.
  2. The cache is the binding constraint. It grows with batch × context and, at long context, exceeds the model weights many times over.
  3. Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, and arithmetic intensity is the reason: too few FLOPs per byte read, so the hardware waits on memory. This is why batching is the primary lever and why the cache is worth shrinking.
  4. The fixes live on different axes — architecture, management, precision, algorithm — and they compose. GQA + PagedAttention + FP8 + FlashAttention is a coherent stack, not four rival ideas.
  5. FLOPs are not wall-clock. The approximate-attention story and the INT4 story are the same cautionary tale from opposite directions.
