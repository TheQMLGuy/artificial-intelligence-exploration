Debate 002

Why not x², ex — or log x?

Is nonlinearity all we need? Three obvious nonlinear functions, three different reasons they fail — and none of them is “because it isn’t nonlinear.”

Status: Open Draft 0.4 Opened 22 Oct 2026 Editor Divyansh Tripathi Draft · Not settled

00Framing: the question carries three assumptions

The question bundles three claims, and all three are false or half-true. Naming them is most of the work.

The three assumptions
  1. “A function is an activation function iff it is nonlinear.” Nonlinearity is necessary but nowhere near sufficient.
  2. “Nonlinearity is everything we need.” No — the shape of the nonlinearity, and how it behaves under composition and gradient flow, matters more than the mere fact of being nonlinear.
  3. “tanh is the only choice, and trigonometry is unexplored.” Both false. There is a whole zoo of activations, and trigonometric activations are a real, published tool — just a specialised one.

So the debate is really: what does an activation function actually have to do, and why do x², ex, log x, and most trigonometric functions fail that test while tanh passes it?

01Why x², ex and log x are not “just other nonlinearities”

It is the one case the theorem excludes

There is a clean, famous result. Leshno, Lin, Pinkus & Schocken (1993): a single-hidden-layer feedforward network is a universal approximator if and only if the activation function is not a polynomial. Polynomial activations are the single class the universal approximation theorem rules out.

That is not a heuristic — it is an iff. x² sits exactly in the excluded class. Hornik’s earlier results had given sufficient conditions (bounded, continuous, non-constant); Leshno et al. sharpened it to the minimal necessary-and-sufficient condition, and “non-polynomial” is it.

The one-line proof

A single hidden layer with activation σ computes:

y = Σj vj · σ(wj·x + bj)

Put σ(t) = t². Each term is vj(wj·x + bj)², and the sum expands to:

y = xTA x + cTx + d

— a quadratic form. So no matter how wide you make the layer, a one-hidden-layer x² network is just a quadratic polynomial in the input. It cannot represent x³, or sin(x), or anything that is not a degree-2 polynomial. Adding width adds nothing; the model class is frozen at “parabolas.”

This is why “it’s nonlinear, so it should work” fails: nonlinearity is not the currency. The currency is whether the family of functions you can build is dense in the space of functions you care about.

Depth does not rescue it — it wrecks it

You might think: fine, stack layers. Composing L quadratic layers gives a polynomial of degree 2L. Polynomials are dense, so asymptotically this is technically a universal approximator again. In practice it is a disaster:

  • Values explode. |x|2L grows doubly-exponentially with depth. Forward activations leave the well-behaved range and overflow to NaN — the “escaping activation problem” documented in the Sisyphus study of polynomial activations.
  • Gradients are ill-conditioned. The derivative is 2x — unbounded, so gradient magnitude depends on how big the activations happen to be. Near zero the gradient vanishes; far from zero it explodes. You get both classic pathologies depending on the region.

It is also lossy and badly shaped

Not injective. x² maps x and −x to the same value. A single unit literally cannot tell a positive pre-activation from its negative — the sign is destroyed. Not monotonic. It goes down then up, with a stationary point at 0. Unbounded above, non-negative range [0, ∞). It can never output a negative value, and it has no ceiling.

But it is not “forbidden” — it is a bad default

Honesty demands the caveat. x² and polynomial activations are used, where the objective is different: privacy-preserving inference replaces ReLU with x² because x² is just a multiply under homomorphic encryption, cutting latency dramatically — the Sisyphus paper exists precisely because practitioners want it to work, and documents why it struggles past a few layers. And recent polynomial / Hermite activations (2025) can train GPT-2 and ConvNeXt, but only with a carefully designed variance-preserving initialisation.

So the accurate statement is: x² is not illegal, it is a poor general-purpose prior. It needs special handling and buys you less expressivity per layer.

The mirror case: why not ex?

The follow-up question is sharper than it looks, because ex passes the very test that x² fails. It is nonlinear, and it is emphatically non-polynomial — so the 1993 theorem’s condition is satisfied, and an ex network is, in principle, a universal approximator. On expressivity, ex is fine.

It also has several properties x² lacks: it is monotonic, injective, smooth, and convex. So if “nonlinear”, or “non-polynomial”, or “monotonic” were the whole story, ex would be a perfectly good activation. It fails on the other axis — conditioning — and it fails harder than x² does:

  • It is unbounded above. ex → ∞. Compose layers and you get an exponential tower: eW₂·eW₁·x. Each layer exponentiates the last, so values overflow to inf after a handful of layers — the exploding-activation problem taken to its extreme.
  • The gradient is the activation. d/dx ex = ex. Wherever the activation is large, the gradient is equally large — exploding gradients, by construction.
  • It vanishes on the other side. As x → −∞, ex → 0 and its gradient → 0. So ex has a vanishing gradient for negative inputs and an exploding one for positive inputs — both classic pathologies in a single function.
  • It is not zero-centred. Its range is (0, ∞): never negative — the same all-positive gradient problem as the logistic sigmoid. Indeed the sigmoid is 1/(1+e−x), so ex is the sigmoid’s raw ingredient.
  • A curiosity: the bias does nothing. Because ew·x + b = eb · ew·x, the threshold term is absorbed into a positive scaling — the bias the 1993 theorem needs for functions like sin is redundant here.

So what did the field do? It did not throw the exponential away — it tamed it. Almost every bounded activation is ex in a wrapper: sigmoid is 1/(1+e−x), tanh is (ex − e−x)/(ex + e−x), softplus is log(1+ex), softmax normalises it, and attention uses a normalised exponential as its kernel. The most direct answer is ELU (Clevert, Unterthiner & Hochreiter, 2015): keep the exponential only on the negative side, where it saturates gracefully, and use the identity on the positive side, where it would explode.

ELU(x) = x   if x > 0
             α(ex − 1)   if x ≤ 0

ELU is, quite literally, “ex with the dangerous half removed.” The set makes the point sharper than any one case alone: x² fails expressivity; ex passes expressivity and fails conditioning. Neither nonlinearity nor non-polynomiality is the currency on its own — conditioning under composition is co-equal. And log x will fail in a third way entirely.

The third case: why not log x?

Log x fails in a way neither of the others does — it is not even defined on half the real line. An activation function must be a map from ℜ to ℜ: it has to accept every real pre-activation — positive, zero, or negative. log x is undefined for x ≤ 0, so any neuron with a non-positive input produces NaN. This is a domain failure, and it is fatal before any question of conditioning even arises.

You can patch the domain, but you then inherit a conditioning problem:

  • Use log(1 + x) (the usual fix): defined for x > −1, but its derivative is 1/(1+x), which → 0 as x → ∞. Vanishing gradients for large inputs — it saturates, one-sided, like a tanh that only flattens on the right.
  • Use log(1 + |x|), or the symmetric log: now defined on all of ℜ, but even (log|−x| = log|x|), so it destroys the sign — the same defect as x². Its derivative, 1/(1+|x|), → 0 at both ends: vanishing gradients everywhere except near zero.
  • Bare log near zero: the derivative 1/x → ∞ as x → 0⁺. So the unpatched function has an exploding gradient at the boundary and a vanishing one at infinity.

It is also unbounded, so it fails the boundedness condition of the original Cybenko universal-approximation theorem (it does satisfy Leshno’s non-polynomial condition, so expressivity is again not the problem).

There is a symmetry worth naming: log x and ex are inverses — mirror images across the line y = x. Where ex explodes as x → +∞, log x explodes as x → 0⁺. Where ex vanishes as x → −∞, log x’s gradient vanishes as x → +∞. They are the same failure, reflected, tamed by the same move: bound the domain and the range.

And again the field tamed it rather than discarding it. Log is everywhere in machine learning — but wrapped:

  • symlog (also called the logarithmic activation function, LAF): the symmetric version, effectively sign(x)·log(1+|x|), used to compress huge dynamic ranges — it is the reward/value transform in Dreamer-style RL agents.
  • softplus = log(1 + ex) — the most famous log-based activation, and itself a composition of the two functions we have been discussing.
  • log-sigmoid = log σ(x) — used as an activation.
  • NLReLU = log(1 + max(0, x)) — log applied to ReLU’s positive side only, keeping ReLU’s sparsity while softening its growth.

The three candidates together form a small taxonomy of why an activation fails — and none of the reasons is “because it isn’t nonlinear”:

That table is the answer to the original question, stated exactly: nonlinearity is the entry ticket, not the qualification.

02So what does an activation function actually have to do?

Nonlinearity is necessary and insufficient. The properties that actually matter, and how the usual suspects score:

Three corrections fall straight out of this table. (1) Non-polynomiality is necessary but not sufficient — it is the exact thing x² lacks, but ex has it and is still unusable. (2) Monotonicity is not required — GELU, Swish and Mish are all non-monotonic and are state of the art. (3) Conditioning beats expressivity in practice — the activation that wins keeps gradients sane through many layers.

A shallow polynomial network is too weak; a deep one is too unstable. That is the whole problem with x² in one sentence.

03Why tanh — and why not (only) the other trigonometric functions?

Why tanh won, historically

  • A bounded, smooth, monotonic squash with range (−1, 1): numerically safe, differentiable everywhere, and it satisfies the sigmoidal universal-approximation conditions.
  • Zero-centred, unlike the logistic sigmoid. This is the real reason it beat sigmoid: if every activation feeding a neuron is positive, the gradient w.r.t. the weights is always all-positive or all-negative, so the weight vector can only move along one diagonal and must “zig-zag” to reach the target. LeCun called this out in Efficient BackProp; tanh removes it.
  • Bigger gradient near the origin. Sigmoid’s derivative peaks at 0.25; tanh’s reaches 1.0 — larger local gradients mean faster learning in the linear regime.
  • It is a rescaled logistic: tanh(x) = 2σ(2x) − 1, so it inherits sigmoid’s guarantees for free.
  • It pairs naturally with gates. In an LSTM, logistic sigmoids gate the flow and tanh squashes the cell state.

But tanh has real weaknesses

It saturates: for large |x| the gradient goes to zero, giving the vanishing-gradient problem in deep stacks. It also costs an exponentiation. Those two facts are exactly why ReLU displaced tanh as the deep-net default — ReLU is monotone, piecewise-linear (gradient 0 or 1, never tiny on the positive side), and dirt cheap. “Why only tanh?” has a one-word answer: it isn’t — it is a legacy default that lost the crown.

The trigonometric question

It is not that trigonometry is forbidden. It is that most trigonometric functions violate the properties in the table:

  • sin and cos are periodic. They map inputs that are far apart to the same output — not injective everywhere, with infinitely many local minima. For a general function approximator on an unbounded domain, periodicity is the wrong prior: it says “the world repeats,” true for signals and false for most everything else.
  • A lovely subtlety. In the 1993 theorem the threshold (bias) is essential. The family {sin(w·x)} contains only odd functions, so without a bias term it cannot even represent cos(x) — an even function. The offset is not a detail; it is what makes the family dense.
  • tan is hopeless as an activation: poles, undefined at π/2, unbounded.

And yet sin is used — where periodicity is the right prior. SIREN (Sitzmann et al., 2020) uses sin as the activation for implicit neural representations: it represents images, audio, wavefields and 3-D shapes and their derivatives far better than ReLU or tanh, and it has an elegant property — the derivative of a SIREN is itself a SIREN (d/dx sin = cos = a phase-shifted sin). It needs a carefully designed initialisation (ω₀ = 30 for the first layer), the price of the periodic prior. And Fourier / trigonometric activations (2025) can train GPT-2 and ConvNeXt using a trigonometric basis.

So the honest answer to “why not other trigonometric ones?” is: sin is not unused — it is specialised. tanh is a general-purpose squash built from exponentials; sin is a periodic prior that wins exactly when the data is periodic. Choosing an activation is choosing an inductive bias, not searching for “any nonlinear function.”

04But Fourier can approximate anything — doesn’t that settle it?

The strongest form of the “why not sin?” objection runs like this: a Fourier series can represent any reasonable function, just as a Taylor series can — and it is built entirely out of sin and cos. So sin and cos are the universal building blocks. Why not use them as activations?

It is a good argument, and following it carefully is instructive — because it turns out to prove something slightly different from what it seems to.

First, Fourier is not Taylor. They are different bases with different domains of validity. A Taylor series is local: it matches derivatives at a point, in the monomial basis {1, x, x², …}, and converges only inside a radius set by the nearest singularity — at a discontinuity the radius collapses to zero and the series cannot even see the jump. A Fourier series is global: its coefficients are integrals over the whole period, in the trigonometric basis, and it converges in L² for any square-integrable function, discontinuities included. The analogy is loose, and the difference matters.

Second — and this is the twist — the Taylor half of the analogy is exactly why x² fails. Take the Taylor route to features and you get polynomials: monomials xⁿ. A polynomial activation gives you precisely a truncated Taylor basis, and we showed in section 01 that a single-hidden-layer x² network collapses to a quadratic form — a Taylor expansion truncated at degree 2. So the Taylor analogy diagnoses the x² failure. The Fourier analogy is the right one instead, because sin is not a polynomial. Followed carefully, the analogy picks out the exact dividing line: polynomial basis → fails; non-polynomial basis → passes.

Third, “a complete basis” is not “a good activation.” These are two different universality statements:

  • Representational universality. The fixed family {1, sin(nx), cos(nx) : n ∈ ℕ} is a complete orthonormal basis of L². But using it means summing countably many fixed terms — for sharp features, very many of them.
  • Learnable universality. The set {Σ vj sin(wjx + bj)} with finitely many, learned frequencies is dense in C(K) — this is Leshno’s theorem, and sin qualifies because it is non-polynomial.

The completeness of the Fourier basis says nothing about activations. The activation’s job is to make a finite, learned network dense — a different theorem. And the reason a network beats a fixed Fourier expansion is that it learns which frequencies matter: it is an adaptive, sparse Fourier representation.

Fourth — the part that actually matters for activations: spectral bias. Here the Fourier connection pays off. Networks trained by gradient descent fit their target from low frequencies to high — a phenomenon called spectral bias (Rahaman et al., 2019) or the frequency principle (Xu et al.). The mechanism is striking: the regularity of the activation function translates into the decay rate of the loss in the frequency domain. A smoother activation ⇒ a more strongly low-frequency-biased network ⇒ high-frequency content is learned slowly, or not at all.

This reframes the trig question entirely. The sinusoid is not a curiosity — it is the basis of the two main cures for spectral bias:

  • Fourier features as input encoding (Tancik et al., 2020): mapping the input through sin/cos at random frequencies turns the network’s effective kernel into a stationary one with a tunable bandwidth, letting it learn high frequencies.
  • sin as the activation (SIREN): a periodic activation, with a deliberately high-frequency first layer, represents high-frequency signals and their derivatives far better than ReLU or tanh.

Where this leaves sin. It is, in fact, the best-conditioned of all our candidates. It is defined on all of ℜ (unlike log x), bounded (unlike x² and ex), smooth, with a bounded derivative (|cos| ≤ 1) — and non-polynomial (unlike x²). It passes every structural test. Its only weakness is the inductive bias: periodicity means aliasing and infinitely many local minima. But that is a soft failure, and a choosable prior — which is exactly why SIREN works.

One caveat, for honesty. “Fourier can approximate anything” is qualified. Convergence is in L², not uniform in general; at a jump discontinuity the partial sums overshoot by about 9% forever (the Gibbs phenomenon); and some continuous functions have Fourier series that diverge at a point. The universality is real but specific.

The net effect: the Fourier argument does not settle the question — but it upgrades sin, from “a specialised prior that happens to work for periodic signals” to “the candidate that passes every structural test and fails only on a soft, choosable prior.”

05What the evidence, read together, suggests

This is a reading, not a verdict. The editor decides what stands.

  1. Nonlinearity is necessary but not sufficient. An activation must also be non-polynomial (or otherwise escape the polynomial trap), numerically well-conditioned under depth, and carry the right inductive bias.
  2. x² fails on the sharpest test and the practical ones. It is the exact class the universal approximation theorem excludes; it is lossy (sign-destroying), unbounded, and ill-conditioned.
  3. ex shows non-polynomiality alone is not enough. It passes the theorem and is still unusable: unbounded, non-zero-centred, and with a gradient equal to its own value — exploding on one side, vanishing on the other. The field’s response was to tame it (sigmoid, tanh, softplus, softmax, ELU), not to discard it.
  4. log x fails in a third way: it is not defined on half of ℜ. A domain failure, not an expressivity or conditioning one — though patched (symlog, log(1+|x|)) it also inherits vanishing gradients. Log survives in machine learning wrapped: symlog, softplus = log(1+ex), log-sigmoid, NLReLU.
  5. Monotonicity is a convenience, not a requirement. The strongest modern activations are non-monotonic.
  6. tanh is a good general squash, not the only one. It won for zero-centredness and a healthy gradient near zero, and it lost the deep-net default to ReLU.
  7. Trigonometric activations are a live tool, not a forbidden zone — sin wins where the signal is periodic, and the bias term is what makes any of it work.
  8. The Fourier argument upgrades sin rather than settling the question. A complete Fourier basis is a representational universality statement, not a statement about activations; and the Taylor half of the analogy is exactly what condemns x². What the Fourier connection really delivers is spectral bias — the regularity of the activation sets the frequency decay of learning — which is why Fourier features and sin activations are the standard cures for it.
